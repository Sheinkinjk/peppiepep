# Peptides in Australia: legal status, what "legalised" could mean, and what Refer Labs can do

Research date: 3 October 2026. Read-only research. No site code changed.
Every primary source below was fetched on 3 October 2026 unless another date is given. Anything marked **[secondary]** comes from a law firm, trade press or news report and not from a regulator. Anything marked **[unverified]** was not confirmed from any source in this session.

---

## 0. The answer in one paragraph

Peptides are not "illegal" in Australia and are not waiting to be "legalised". Most of the ones people mean are **already legal on prescription**: they are Schedule 4 (prescription-only), several also carry the Appendix D clause 5 possession control, none is on the ARTG, and they reach patients through doctor prescriptions filled by compounding pharmacies under the one-patient compounding exemption. The legal change people picture (pharmacies or telehealth openly selling BPC-157 the way the US may soon allow) has **no sign of happening here**. No scheduling proposal, consultation, ARTG application or inquiry for any of these peptides was open or listed on 3 October 2026. Every signal since April 2026 points the other way: a TGA safety advisory (13 April), a compliance priority (10 June), a joint TGA and Chief Medical Officer warning (19 June), new advertising guidance that names **"peptides"** as a banned class reference (18 June), a "put on notice" warning aimed at affiliates by name (20 July) and a $120,000 raid on an influencer (4 August). The FDA's July 2026 advisory vote has no legal effect in Australia, and on compounding Australia went the opposite way to the US in 2024. For a publisher the rule is decisive: **the word "peptides" in a promotional context is itself listed by the TGA as prohibited advertising**, so the "be early" play cannot be built on the peptide keyword at all.

**What "not scheduled" does and does not mean.** An unscheduled peptide (GHK-Cu injectable, epitalon, MOTS-c, retatrutide) is not "legal to sell". Any therapeutic good must be on the ARTG or exempt before it is imported, manufactured or supplied, and the TGA says "Supplying unapproved goods where no exemption applies is unlawful. This includes compounded medicines." (TGA, *Manufacturing, supplying and advertising compounded medicines lawfully*, published 14 May 2026, updated 8 Sep 2026, https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/manufacturing-supplying-and-advertising-compounded-medicines-lawfully). Advertising unapproved goods to the public is also unlawful. Scheduling decides *who may supply and possess*; ARTG status decides *whether it may be supplied at all*.

**How BPC-157 got scheduled (the template for any future peptide).** TGA, *Notice of interim decisions*, ACMS #43 (November 2023 meeting), https://www.tga.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-04/public-notice-of-interim-decisions-acms-43-accs-37-joint-acms-accs-35.pdf: proposed by the Delegate after "48 referrals for importation of BPC-157 received by the TGA since 1 July 2022"; interim decision to create a Schedule 4 and Appendix D clause 5 entry, implementation **1 June 2024**; 22 public submissions, essentially all supportive. Reasons included that vendors' "'not for therapeutic use' or 'for research only'" labels were "insufficient to deter consumer misuse". BPC-157 sits in the June 2026 instrument, so the decision was finalised. Several "2025" and "2023" dates for BPC-157 in search results and peptide blogs are wrong.

### 1.2 Compounding: what pharmacies may still do

- **Exemption.** Schedule 5 item 6 of the Therapeutic Goods Regulations 1990 exempts a medicine from ARTG inclusion when it is "dispensed or extemporaneously compounded, for a particular person for therapeutic application to that person". For an S4 medicine this requires a prescription and the patient's request *before* compounding. Schedule 8 of the Regulations exempts pharmacists and medical practitioners from a manufacturing licence on conditions. (TGA compounding guidance above, and TGA safety advisory *Understanding your responsibilities when importing, compounding and supplying unapproved peptide products*, 13 April 2026, https://www.tga.gov.au/safety/safety-monitoring-and-information/safety-alerts/understanding-your-responsibilities-when-importing-compounding-and-supplying-unapproved-peptide-products.)
- **Excluded from the exemption:** gene therapy, biologicals, medicinal cannabis products and **GLP-1 receptor agonist analogues**. "From 1 October 2024, pharmacists must not compound medicines containing GLP-1 receptor agonist analogues." (Same guidance; and TGA media release 18 June 2024, https://www.tga.gov.au/news/media-releases/update-glucagon-peptide-1-receptor-agonists-glp-1-ras-pharmacy-compounding-changes, which says the Schedule 5 item 6 amendment applies "regardless of dosage form".) The GLP-1 change is the precedent for how a peptide compounding crackdown would be done: an amendment to the Regulations with roughly a four-month lead-in after it was made.
- **So, today:** a pharmacist may lawfully compound BPC-157, CJC-1295, ipamorelin etc. for one named patient on a prescription, subject to state law, Pharmacy Board standards and the TGA's warnings. The TGA told *Australian Pharmacist* (31 Aug 2026) that there are "significant uncertainties associated with compounded unapproved peptide products (such as BPC-157), particularly where the active ingredient, dosage form or formulation is not described in recognised compounding references" **[secondary: trade press quoting a TGA spokesperson]**, https://www.australianpharmacist.com.au/the-peptide-surge-has-reached-compounding/. The Conversation (21 July 2026) describes online clinics making "the same recipe in bulk" labelled per patient as "really manufacturing, which needs a licence" **[secondary]**, https://theconversation.com/how-wellness-clinics-bend-the-rules-to-supply-unapproved-peptides-and-anti-ageing-injections-287884. That is the line the TGA would draw in an enforcement case.
- **Advertising compounded medicines:** "Although compounded medicines are exempt from some requirements under the Act, they are not exempt from the advertising requirements." (TGA, *Advertising health services that involve therapeutic goods*, updated 18 June 2026.) Precedent: **Your Solution Compounding Pharmacy** gave a court-enforceable undertaking (TGA release 7 April 2025) after its website carried order forms for semaglutide and tirzepatide; "compounding pharmacies are permitted to promote their compounding services to consumers, they are prohibited from advertising the use or supply of prescription medicines." https://www.tga.gov.au/news/media-releases/your-solution-compounding-pharmacy-undertakes-comply-advertising-rules-weight-loss-medicines

### 1.3 Importation

- **Personal Importation Scheme** (TGA page, updated 8 Sep 2026, https://www.tga.gov.au/products/unapproved-therapeutic-goods/personal-importation-scheme): for a prescription-only medicine "you must hold a valid Australian prescription or written authority at the time of importation", no more than a 3-month supply, for the importer or immediate family only, never resold. Some substances are Customs-controlled and need an import permit.
- **Peptide-specific:** "Importing unapproved peptide products under the Personal Importation Scheme is permitted only if the goods are clearly and accurately labelled". Code-only or unlabelled vials "will not be released", and "we will notify the ABF to seize and destroy these products" (TGA safety advisory, 7 May 2026, https://www.tga.gov.au/safety/safety-monitoring-and-information/safety-alerts/tga-warning-risks-importing-unapproved-peptide-products). "Disclaimers that a product is for 'research use only' does not make its supply lawful" (13 April 2026 advisory).
- Some GH-axis peptides are said to be Customs-controlled imports needing a permit a patient cannot get (The Conversation, 21 July 2026) **[secondary; the Customs (Prohibited Imports) Regulations schedule was not read]**.

**Plain statement: there is no sign of liberalisation in Australia.** The live risk runs the other way: (a) a Regulations amendment removing named peptides, or all unapproved injectable peptides, from the Schedule 5 item 6 compounding exemption (the GLP-1 route); (b) scheduling of the peptides not yet named (GHK-Cu injectable, retatrutide, MOTS-c, epitalon) into S4 or Appendix D, the way BPC-157 was done after import referrals piled up; (c) more advertising prosecutions. None of these has started publicly, so none has a date. If one starts, the BPC-157 timetable (Nov 2023 meeting, interim decision published April 2024, in force 1 June 2024) and the GLP-1 timetable (made by 18 June 2024, in force 1 October 2024) give a guide of roughly 4 to 8 months from first public notice.

### 2.2 United States: real movement, advisory only

- **Category 2 removals.** The FDA's Category 2 page (content current as of 22 Apr 2026, https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/certain-bulk-drug-substances-use-compounding-may-present-significant-safety-risks) now lists under "nominated but withdrawn": AOD-9604, BPC-157, CJC-1295, dihexa, emideltide (DSIP), epitalon, GHK-Cu (injectable), KPV, Melanotan II, MOTS-c, selank, semax, thymosin alpha-1, TB-500 and others. **Still in Category 2:** ipamorelin (503B), kisspeptin-10 (503A), ibutamoren. McDermott Will & Schulte (27 Jul 2026) says HHS Secretary Kennedy announced the review on 27 Feb 2026 and confirmed 12 removals on 15 Apr 2026, and that "removal from Category 2 does not automatically confer Category 1 status" **[secondary]**.
- **PCAC meeting.** Federal Register notice 2026-07361 (published 16 Apr 2026, docket FDA-2025-N-6895), https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-07361.pdf: on 23 July the committee reviewed BPC-157 (for ulcerative colitis), KPV (wound healing and inflammatory conditions), TB-500 (wound healing) and MOTS-c (obesity and osteoporosis); on 24 July emideltide, semax and epitalon. FDA staff had proposed against adding BPC-157 **[secondary, search summary of FDA briefing doc]**.
- **Vote.** The committee recommended BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, semax and epitalon and rejected emideltide (McDermott, 27 Jul 2026) **[secondary]**. Reported tallies: 8-6 with 1 abstention for BPC-157, KPV and TB-500; 7-5 with 2 abstentions for MOTS-c (AJMC and others) **[secondary]**.
- **Not yet law.** A PCAC vote is advisory. FDA must run notice-and-comment rulemaking to add a substance to the 503A list; McDermott expects informal enforcement discretion in the meantime **[secondary]**. No final or proposed FDA rule was found as of this date **[unverified: no Federal Register search for a proposed rule was made]**.

**Does Australia follow the FDA here?** No, and the evidence is direct. On the same class of product, Australia removed GLP-1 analogues from compounding in 2024 while the US was still permitting shortage compounding. Australian scheduling decisions are made by a TGA delegate on ACMS advice and cite Australian import referrals, not FDA lists (the BPC-157 decision does not mention the FDA). The likely Australian effect of US liberalisation is *more* demand, more imports and more grey-market promotion, which is the trigger for *more* Australian enforcement.

## 3. The structural point: confirmed, and stricter than the brief assumed

**Confirmed:** if a peptide is or becomes S4, it cannot be advertised to the public. TGA, *Complying with the restrictions on advertising prescription medicines to the public* (published 18 June 2026, updated 23 June 2026, https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/complying-restrictions-advertising-prescription-medicines-public), lists as prohibited references:
- direct substances, with **ipamorelin** as an example;
- abbreviations, with **"BPC 157, CJC 1295"** as examples;
- "Reference to a class of prescription medicines, including indirectly or using substitute terms", with **"Peptides"** as an example;
- images of vials or syringes.

It also says health services "that present themselves as services for prescribing or supplying specific prescription medicines risk unlawfully promoting those medicines", naming **"peptides"** among the examples, and that a form letting consumers "check their eligibility for a prescription medicine" is advertising. Linking to third-party material that promotes a prescription medicine can make the linking business's own material an advertisement. The health-services guidance (updated 18 June 2026, https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/advertising-health-services-involve-therapeutic-goods) adds: "Promoting a telehealth service as a way to obtain particular prescription medicines or a class of prescription medicines is likely to constitute prohibited advertising."

**Corrections to the brief:**
1. It is stricter than "can't promote the product". **The category word itself is prohibited.** A "peptide therapy" page, a "peptide clinic" comparison or a "peptides waitlist" with an affiliate link would each be advertising a class of prescription medicines. Peptide Clinics was fined partly *for using the word*.
2. Ahpra s133 does **not** ban inducements outright. s133(1)(b) bans offering "a gift, discount or other inducement ... unless the advertisement also states the terms and conditions of the offer". **Testimonials are banned outright** (s133(1)(c)), as are misleading claims and creating "an unreasonable expectation of beneficial treatment". (Ahpra advertising guidelines, https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub/Advertising-guidelines-and-other-guidance/Advertising-guidelines.aspx.) s133 applies to "a person", so it binds Refer Labs when advertising a regulated health service, not only the practitioner.
3. "Unscheduled" peptides (GHK-Cu injectable, epitalon, MOTS-c, retatrutide) are not a loophole. They are unapproved goods, and advertising unapproved goods to the public is "generally unlawful" (TGA, 20 July 2026).

### 3.1 Lawful business models if a peptide is available on prescription, ranked

Ranked by how realistic and defensible each is for Refer Labs.

**1. Affiliate or referral partner of a general longevity or men's or women's health clinic, promoting the consultation only. (Realistic now, low capital, but it is not a "peptides" business.)**
- What it is: the Moshy and Mosh model. You promote "a consultation with a registered practitioner about energy, recovery or healthy ageing, who decides whether any treatment is appropriate", and never peptides, never named molecules, never vial imagery.
- Requires: the partner to be a lawful clinic (AHPRA-registered prescribers, a real consult rather than a questionnaire **[the Medical Board's telehealth prescribing guidance was not re-read this session]**); the partner's landing page to be clean too, because the link carries its content back to us (see the Mosh and Moshy landing-page problem in `tga-service-wording-2026-09-30.md`); s133 compliance (terms beside any discount, no testimonials, no outcome expectations); affiliate disclosure; ACL s29.
- The catch: a clinic whose brand or product *is* peptides (a name like "Pepti Clinic") cannot be promoted without the promotion itself naming the class. Only a clinic with a broader service identity works.

**2. Equity stake in a lawful telehealth clinic. (Possible, capital-heavy, regulatory risk sits with the clinic.)**
- Requires: the clinic to hold AHPRA-registered prescribers employing their own clinical judgement; a partner pharmacy licensed under state law and compliant with Pharmacy Board compounding standards, or an ARTG product; no prescribing on questionnaire alone; marketing that promotes the service without the class word; a TGA-literate lawyer. Owning a stake does not let Refer Labs promote the clinic any more freely, and a related-party commercial interest would need disclosure on every page.
- Risk: scenario B (compounding restriction) would remove the product line overnight, as it did for compounded GLP-1s on 1 October 2024.

**3. Owning the clinic outright. (Not recommended.)** Everything in 2 plus the operator's direct liability for advertising, prescribing governance and pharmacy supply, in a priority enforcement area. Peptide Clinics went into liquidation during its case (The Conversation) **[secondary]**.

**Not lawful under any scenario:** affiliate links to peptide vendors or "research peptide" sites; sourcing or "where to buy" content; any page that ranks peptides, compares peptide clinics by peptide, or offers a "check if you're eligible for peptides" tool; any discount code tied to peptides.

## 5. What "early" can lawfully look like now

### 5.1 Topical cosmetic peptides (GHK-Cu serums, Matrixyl): yes, with cosmetic claims only

The TGA's boundary guidance (*Determining if your product is a cosmetic or therapeutic good*, updated 3 July 2026, https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/determining-if-your-product-cosmetic-or-therapeutic-good) turns on the claim:
- Cosmetic: "our facial oil contains collagen to smooth the appearance of fine lines".
- Therapeutic: "stimulates collagen production to repair skin at the cellular level ... smooth out wrinkles from within".
- Products that "promote hair growth or decrease/reduce hair loss" are likely therapeutic goods.

So a copper-peptide serum is a cosmetic (ACCC and ACL, ingredients under AICIS) **if** the copy stays on appearance, hydration and texture. Copy about repair, regeneration, collagen stimulation or hair regrowth makes it a therapeutic good, and an unlisted one. This fits the existing Health & Beauty hub and its CF partners. Two cautions:
- **Do not use "peptides" as the page's category word** (for example a "Best peptides" page). Use the ingredient name on the product ("copper tripeptide-1", "palmitoyl pentapeptide-4", "Matrixyl") inside skincare content. An injectable-peptide searcher landing on a "peptides" page next to affiliate links is the exact class-reference problem.
- Keep it inside a skincare comparison with a primary-sourced owned fact, per the citation rule. Do not build a standalone "peptide serums" best-X page.

### 5.2 Collagen peptides (food): low value, low risk

Collagen powders are generally sold as foods, so FSANZ food standards apply, and health claims are limited to those Standard 1.2.7 permits **[not re-read this session]**. Lawful to cover, but it captures none of the injectable-peptide demand and has no obvious partner.

### 5.3 Educational content and waitlist: very narrow, and not recommended in the shape proposed

- An educational, non-commercial explainer ("Are peptides legal in Australia? What the TGA says") would be the only content that can use the word, and only if the page carries **no affiliate link, no clinic link, no CTA and no internal link into a page that has one**. CLAUDE.md already records why that is hard on this site: "Every page on this site carries an affiliate CTA or links into one, so no page can claim the editorial exemption". Even the global header and footer that funnel into money pages put this at risk; it needs a lawyer's view before it is built.
- **Waitlist:** a "join the peptides waitlist" capture is promotion of a class of prescription medicines whose monetisation event is a future sale. It fails the TGA test and the revenue-first rule. Do not build it.
- The citation-rule angle does exist: the owned fact, a correct primary-sourced schedule table (Section 1.1) where peptide blogs still carry wrong BPC-157 dates, would be citable. But it would have no lawful monetisation route, so under CLAUDE.md's revenue-first rule it should not ship as a page.
